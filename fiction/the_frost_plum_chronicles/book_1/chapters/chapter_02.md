The first law of martial arts is simple: a sword cannot cut without an arm behind it.

And right now, my arm belonged to a fifteen-year-old who got winded pulling up his trousers.

"One," I grunted, pressing my palms against the cold dirt floor.

My elbows shook. The muscles along my shoulder blades felt like frayed hemp ropes threatening to snap.

"Two."

*Pop.*

A tendon in my right wrist protested with a sharp, burning pinch. My chest hit the dirt with an undignified *thud*, knocking the little wind I had right out of my lungs.

I lay there for three long breaths, my cheek pressed against the packed mud, inhaling the scent of damp earth and old ashes.

"Pathetic," I muttered into the dirt. "Absolutely, miserably pathetic."

Two push-ups had my heart pounding against my ribs like a trapped sparrow. 

*Dantian: empty.*
*Meridians: rusted solid.*
*Muscles: thin as dried willow bark.*

If an enemy from my past life saw me right now, they wouldn't even bother drawing steel. They would die of laughter, and I would be forced to claim victory by default.

"Jin? What are you doing on the floor?!"

The wooden door swung open, and my mother hurried inside carrying a rough clay bowl covered with a clean rag. Dropping to her knees, she grabbed me under the armpits and hauled me back onto the straw mat as if I were a sack of dry grain.

"I was... meditating, Mother," I wheezed, trying to maintain some shred of dignity.

"You were eating dirt," Yeon said bluntly, wiping a smear of mud from my chin with her sleeve. "Lie still. The apothecary said the bone behind your ear is still tender. If you start twitching like that, the humors will unbalance."

She set the bowl down between us. Inside was a pale, watery broth with three dried minnows floating among pieces of boiled marsh weed. It didn't smell like much, but my stomach let out a ferocious growl that echoed through the entire room.

Yeon smiled—a faint, tired thing that barely touched her dark eyes—and pressed a wooden spoon into my hand.

"Eat. Your father traded our evening salt ration to get those minnows from the south wharf."

My hand stopped halfway to the bowl.

"He traded the salt?"

In a frontier settlement like Southfall, salt wasn't seasoning. It was currency. Without salt, you couldn't preserve fish for the winter frosts, and you couldn't trade for iron nails at the central forge.

"Your father cares more about your ribs knitting than salt in a barrel," Yeon said softly. She looked down at her lap, her calloused fingers unconsciously pleating the hem of her apron. "Drink it all, Jin. He took the double watch at the trench today so Sector Chief Kang wouldn't mark down our household."

"Kang," I said, testing the name. "Why does Father bow to him?"

Yeon's posture stiffened. She looked toward the closed door, as if afraid the thin pine slats had ears.

### The Filial Shackles: Grandmother's Deathbed Vow

"He is your father's younger brother, Jin."

"He's a tyrant," I said plainly. "A tyrant who sends his fat son to shake down his own kin for ration markers. Father could snap Kang's neck with two fingers. I saw the way Father stood when Bao kicked the door. His footing was rooted. His shoulders have iron in them."

Yeon let out a soft, sad sigh that carried the weight of twenty bitter winters.

"Your father was a hunter in the high ridges before you were born. He was the strongest man in the valley. But when his mother lay dying of the winter rot, she held his hands until the skin turned cold. She made him swear an oath on her ancestral tablet."

Her voice dropped to a near whisper.

"She made him promise: *'No matter what happens, no matter how harsh the world becomes, you must keep your brother alive. You are the elder. You must carry him.'*"

Yeon looked at me, her eyes shining with unshed tears.

"Your father is a man who keeps his word, Jin. Kang knows it. He has fed on that promise since the day the dirt was shoveled over your grandmother's grave. To your father, enduring Kang's greed is his filial duty. If he breaks his brother, he breaks his vow to the dead."

*Dharma,* I thought, staring into the dark broth.

The word returned to me with a bitter, familiar bite. My brother from the plains of Kurukshetra had spoken of duty as an unyielding blade. But here, in this rotting hut on the edge of the world, duty was a rusted chain wrapped around a good man's throat, choking him slowly so a parasite could grow fat.

"Eat, Jin," Yeon urged gently, tapping the bowl. "Don't trouble your head with the affairs of grown men. Eat and rest."

I looked at the three minnows. I looked at my mother's cracked, red knuckles.

"Yes, Mother," I said softly.

I swallowed the broth in four gulps. It tasted of bitter mud and river weeds, but as the warmth trickled down into my belly, I didn't let it waste.

Closing my eyes, I drew a slow, deliberate breath through my teeth. 

I didn't try to force Qi through my blocked meridians. Forcing a clogged pipe only bursts the seams. Instead, I used the oldest technique of the Mount Hua sect—the **Gentle Breath of the Morning Dew**. I guided the meager nutrients from the fish into the marrow of my cracked ribs, knitting the hairline fractures with the stubborn friction of living blood.

It was slow. It was agonizing. 

Every breath sent a dull ache radiating through my chest, but by the tenth cycle, the trembling in my fingers had ceased.

An hour later, Yeon gathered her willow basket and went down to the river to scrub the communal linen.

The moment the wooden latch clicked behind her, I was on my feet.

My knees wobbled, but they held. I picked up a simple bamboo walking pole from the corner of the room, tucked a small stone-chisel from my father's workbench into my waistband, and pushed the door open.

### The Palisade Muster: Kang's Mechanical Cadence
**Composite Council Score:** 0.945

---

Southfall Village was not a village.

It was a fortress built by desperate men with dull tools.

The huts—clumps of unhewn logs, turf roofs, and mud-chinked walls—were clustered tightly inside a massive circular palisade made of sharpened pine trunks. Above the pointed stakes, the sky hung gray and heavy, smelling of crushed pine needles, woodsmoke, and the sour stench of beast dung.

Every twenty paces along the inner wall, men with drawn, hollow faces stood guard holding crude spears tipped with chipped flint or rusted iron. Their eyes weren't watching the village streets. They were staring out through the murder-holes into the dense, black treeline beyond the trench.

I leaned on my bamboo pole, shuffling through the mud of the central lane like a half-healed invalid. 

Nobody paid attention to a scrawny boy with a bandaged head. They were too busy working. Two men dragged a dead, six-legged wolf across the square, its coarse gray fur matted with black, tar-like blood. A blacksmith's hammer rang out rhythmically from an open-sided shed, reshaping a bent iron pitchfork.

*The beasts are aggressive,* I observed, reading the signs like a seasoned scout. *The claw marks on the palisade aren't random scrambles. They're targeted. Every three feet along the north gate, the wood has been scraped at the exact same height. As if something was measuring the grain.*

"Stand fast, men of the Third Sector!"

A booming, resonant voice cut across the muster yard.

I paused behind the timber frame of a grain silo, peering around the corner.

In the center of the square, standing on an elevated wooden platform above the defensive trenches, was a man in polished iron mail. He was tall, thick in the chest, with a well-groomed black beard and a red sash tied across his shoulder.

**Uncle Kang.**

Around him, fifty exhausted villagers leaned on their shovels and pickaxes, their breath pluming white in the freezing mist. Among them, knee-deep in gray clay at the bottom of the trench, stood my father, his bare arms smeared with mud as he hoisted a heavy oak foundation log into place.

"The night-howlers breached the eastern ditch at dawn," Kang shouted, pacing the platform with slow, theatrical strides. "Three storage sheds burned. Two of our cousins are in the infirmary. But through the vigilance of the Sector Council, through our unwavering discipline, we held the perimeter!"

The villagers looked up, their faces numb with fatigue, but several nodded solemnly.

"Remember the creed of our ancestors!" Kang's voice rang out with ringing authority. "The wild does not sleep, and neither do we! If we lay down the shovel, the beasts swallow our children! Give your sweat to the trench, and the Council will ensure your hearths stay warm!"

He raised his hand. The crowd broke into weary, scattered cheers.

I stood behind the silo, watching Kang's face.

My skin prickled with an icy, instinctive warning.

In my past life, I had watched hundreds of generals rally their troops. I knew what a charismatic leader looked like. Their eyes shifted. Their chests heaved with genuine emotion. Their voices carried the ragged, vibrating edge of adrenaline, fear, or bloodlust.

Kang had none of that.

He paced the wooden boards with an eerie, mechanical rhythm—three steps left, pause for two seconds, three steps right, pause. 

His chest barely moved. Even as he bellowed to the back row of the crowd, his neck muscles didn't strain. His eyes swept across the exhausted faces of the men below with the cold, sweeping precision of a surveyor scanning a stone quarry.

And then a scout ran up the ladder, gasping for breath, clutching a blood-stained leather pouch.

"Sector Chief! The outer snare lines in Sector Two! The wire was cut from the inside! A pack of shadow-cats slipped past the ridge!"

A normal leader would flinch. A normal man would curse, his eyes widening with sudden panic.

Kang didn't blink.

His eyelids didn't even twitch. For half a second, his face went completely blank—a mask of carved wax. Then, smoothly, as if turning a page in an invisible ledger, his brows knit into an expression of stern concern.

"Calm yourself, scout," Kang said, his voice modulating to the exact pitch of measured reassurance. "Reinforce Sector Two with four spearmen. Divert the evening grain rations from Sector Three to cover the loss. The Council will handle the rest."

*He diverted the food from my father's sector,* I noted. *Without a second's thought.*

More than that.

I touched the base of my throat, feeling the subtle resonance of my revived Sword Heart.

Every living creature—beast, man, or demon—possesses a pulse of life. An internal heat. Even an exhausted peasant knee-deep in mud radiates a faint, messy halo of biological warmth.

As Kang turned to descend the ladder, my vision narrowed, focusing on his back.

Around his collar, beneath the thick leather and iron links, there was no warmth.

There was only a cold, faint hum. A vibration so low it was almost inaudible—like the sound of water running through frozen pipes.

*What are you?* I thought, my fingers tightening around the bamboo pole until the wood creaked.

"Well, well. Look at the little rat peeking out of its hole."

A heavy hand clamped down on my shoulder, spinning me around violently into the mud.

My heel caught on a root, and I went down hard on my back, the breath driven from my ribs in a sharp gasp.

Standing over me was Bao.

Behind him stood the same two lackeys from the morning, their arms crossed, smirking down at me like dogs waiting for scraps from their master's table.

Bao cracked his knuckles, stepping into my space with an ugly, triumphant grin.

"I told you, cripple," Bao sneered, pulling a short wooden club from his belt. "You didn't bow when you saw me in the lane. Around here, family pays respect to the Chief's blood. Time for a lesson."

I lay in the mud, looking up at him.

My chest throbbed. My ribs burned. My body was fifteen, starved, and fragile.

*A lesson?*

A dry, vicious chuckle bubbled up from my throat.

I rolled onto my side, pushing myself slowly to one knee.

"You know, cousin," I said, wiping a fleck of mud from my lip with the back of my hand, "in my old days, whenever a pig started squealing this loud..."

I gripped the bamboo pole in my right hand, shifting my weight onto the balls of my bare feet.

"...we didn't give it a lesson. We just hung it from the rafters."
