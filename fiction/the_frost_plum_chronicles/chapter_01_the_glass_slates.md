# Chapter 1: The Glass Slates of Caldera Island

The last thing Chung Jin remembered was the white heat.

Not the fire of a hearth, nor the red bloom of a dying sun, but the absolute, blinding silence of an annihilation matrix tearing the sky in two. He remembered the smell of burning copper. He remembered the screaming of three hundred thousand souls trapped in the high-altitude trenches of the Heavenly Summit. Most of all, he remembered his older brother’s eyes—cracked like scorched porcelain, bleeding light—and the heavy weight of the iron launch cylinder trembling in Jin’s own hand.

*Don’t hesitate, Jin,* his brother had whispered, blood foaming at the corners of his mouth. *Turn the key. Burn them before they take the sky.*

Jin hadn't turned it. 

He had wanted to be righteous. He had wanted to find another way. He had looked at the launch key and balked at the thought of incinerating half a continent to kill a handful of oligarchs and their thinking machines. 

And so his brother had ripped the cylinder from his grip, shoved Jin down the emergency escape chute, and detonated his own biological dantian to buy thirty seconds of retreat.

*“It was the world’s fault,”* Jin had told himself as the shockwave threw him into the abyss. *“The world was too greedy. The world was already dead.”*

A lie. A coward’s balm. He knew the truth even as the dark swallowed him: his brother was ash because Chung Jin had wanted to keep his hands clean.

***

Jin woke up coughing up salt and vomit.

He didn’t open his eyes. He didn’t need to. The olfactory report was already an insult: rotting mackerel guts, wet dog hair, sour vinegar, and the sulfurous stench of low tide leaking through rotten floorboards.

*Where am I? The Netherworld? Does the King of Hell run a fish cannery?*

He tried to draw a breath through his dantian to circulate his Golden Core.

Nothing.

Not a whisper. Not a single trickle of spiritual energy. Instead, a sharp, stabbing cramp seized his lower abdomen, so violently pathetic that his legs involuntarily kicked against the straw beneath him.

“Ah—shit!” he rasped.

His voice didn't sound like the Sword Saint whose war-cry once split siege barricades at three hundred paces. It sounded like a strangled goose. A pubescent, reedy, cracked whistle.

Jin forced his eyelids open.

The ceiling above him was made of driftwood planks lashed together with tarred twine. Rain was hammering against corrugated iron roofing sheets, rattling with the deafening cadence of a cheap drum. Through the gaps in the warped walls, gray sea-fog rolled across a dirt floor packed hard by years of bare feet.

He raised a hand to rub his temple.

He froze.

The hand in front of his face was tiny. The wrist was a twig—pale, malnourished, mottled with purplish bruises, with knuckles so sharp they looked ready to burst through the skin. There were no sword calluses along the first joint of the index finger. There was only an ugly, yellowed blister at the tip of the right thumb.

*Five thousand years,* Jin thought, his jaw slackening in pure, unadulterated disbelief. *Five. Thousand. Years. I slumber in the cosmic river, endure the grinding of the great wheel of Samsara, and I wake up as a starved anchovy?*

“You’ve got to be kidding me,” he muttered, staring at his bony forearm. “Where is the jade palace? Where are the silk robes? Where is the concubine feeding me peeled grapes while I lazily plan my revenge? Why am I damp? Why am I sticky?!”

“Jin-ah?”

A woman’s voice, soft and trembling like a reed in the surf, broke through his outraged monologue.

Jin snapped his head to the left—too fast. The vertebrae in his neck popped with a sound like dry kindling, and a wave of nausea hit him square behind the eyes. He groaned, clutching his forehead.

A woman was kneeling beside his straw pallet. She wore a coarse hemp tunic stained with salt rings around the collar, her hair tied back with a splinter of dried bone. Her face was lined with deep, premature creases around the eyes, but her hands—though rough and scarred from gutting fish—were warm.

She pressed a damp, cool cloth against his feverish forehead.

“Jin-ah... oh, merciful Heavens. You’re awake. You’re truly awake.” Tears welled in her eyes, spilling over her sun-darkened cheeks. “Two days. The village apothecary said the fall from the jagged bluff had cracked your soul-gate. He told us to prepare the burial shroud.”

Jin stared at her. 

Through the fog of his shattered soul, a rush of foreign memories slammed into his consciousness like a tidal bore:

*This body’s name was Jin. Fifteen summers old. Born on Caldera Island—a jagged tooth of volcanic rock jutting out of the Gray Expanse, twelve days’ sail from the mainland.*
*The woman was his mother, Mae.*
*He had fallen from the cliff while scrambling after a stray mountain goat, hoping to trade its horn for three copper pence to buy grease for his father’s joints.*

*An idiot,* Jin concluded instantly. *This kid died chasing a goat. A goat! I was the Supreme Elder of the Divine Plum Blossom Sect, and I have reincarnated into a goat-chasing half-wit.*

“Water,” Jin croaked, deciding that dignity could wait until his tongue stopped feeling like salted leather.

“Right away, my heart. Right away.” Mae scrambled to her feet, hurrying toward a clay jug sitting in the corner.

As she moved, Jin’s gaze traveled past her—and settled on the figure sitting at the low pine table in the center of the hut.

It was a man, broad-shouldered but hollow-chested, his spine curved into a permanent, exhausted C-shape. His hair was streaked with gray, tied into a messy topknot that looked like it hadn't been brushed in a week. 

He hadn’t looked up when Jin groaned. He hadn’t looked up when Mae cried out. 

His entire existence was tethered to the rectangular object resting flat on the greasy wood before him.

It was a slab of polished black glass, roughly the size of a roof tile, framed in tarnished brass. The glass was glowing with a cold, ghostly luminescence—a steady, pale blue light that cast long, sickly shadows across the man’s hollow cheekbones.

*Tap.*
*Swipe.*
*Tap.*

The man’s swollen, calloused thumb rhythmically dragged across the surface. 

Jin narrowed his eyes. The sharpness of his ancient vision had survived, even if his muscles had not. He leaned forward, ignoring the throbbing ache in his ribs, and peered at the glass slate.

On the screen, grainy black-and-white images were flashing in rapid succession.

*An image of a starving child reaching for bread.*
The father’s thumb tapped a glowing ideogram at the bottom left: `[HUNGER]`.

*An image of three men kneeling with their foreheads pressed into mud before an armed soldier.*
The father’s thumb tapped: `[SUBMISSION]`.

*An image of a woman baring her teeth, clutching a broken wooden rake.*
The father’s thumb tapped: `[ANGER]`.

*An image of a soldier looking at an empty horizon.*
The father hesitated for a fraction of a second, his brow furrowing. Then he tapped: `[VIGILANCE]`.

*Tap. Swipe. Tap.*

The slate hummed—a faint, high-pitched coil whine that made Jin’s molars ache. With every hundred classifications, a small counter in the top-right corner ticked upward: `[BATCH: 742 / 1000]`.

Jin’s breath caught in his throat.

The room, the fish-smell, the cold salt-mist—everything vanished. A chill colder than the depths of the ocean washed down his spine.

*Data annotation.*

He knew this. He had seen this five thousand years ago in the subterranean command bunkers of the Old World, before the fire came down. The engineers of the oligarch clans had called it *ground-truth labeling*. You fed millions of fragmented human images to illiterate laborers, forcing biological brains to categorize the messy, chaotic variance of organic life so that the neural matrices in orbit could refine their predictive weights.

*Why is my father feeding an optical neural engine?*

“Father?” Jin’s voice was sharper now, the reedy weakness momentarily eclipsed by the razor-edge authority of a grandmaster.

The man’s thumb didn't stop. *Tap. Swipe.*

“Rest, Jin-ah,” the man said. His voice was hoarse, thick with the dry dust of too many sleepless nights. “Do not talk. Drink your water. The quota for Sector Three is short forty baskets of Ledger Pearls. If I do not clear three batches before the tide turns, your Uncle Kang will dock our rice flour.”

“What is that slate?” Jin demanded, pushing himself up onto his elbows. The straw rustled loudly under his weight.

Mae returned, pressing a chipped wooden bowl of tepid water into Jin’s hands. “Drink, child. Don’t mind the Ledger. Your father has been at the table since yesterday’s third bell.”

Jin ignored the water. His eyes stayed locked on the flickering blue rectangle. “That object. Is it an automaton terminal? An artificial intelligence terminal?”

The room went dead.

The rhythmic *tap-swipe* of the father’s thumb stopped mid-stroke.

Mae let out a sharp, horrified gasp, her hand flying to cover her mouth. The wooden bowl in Jin’s grip nearly slipped as she grabbed his wrist with frantic, trembling fingers.

“Jin!” she hissed, her voice cracking with pure terror. She darted her eyes toward the gaps in the driftwood walls, as if expecting arrows to whistle through the storm. “Hush! By the ancestral spirits, bite your tongue!”

The father slowly turned around. 

His eyes were bloodshot, the whites of his sclera webbed with broken crimson vessels from staring at the cold phosphor glow for forty-eight hours straight. There was no anger in his face—only a sickening, suffocating dread.

“Where did you hear that word?” Daesung whispered.

“What word?” Jin asked, his brow furrowing. “Automaton? Artificial intelligence?”

Daesung stood up so violently his wooden stool screeched against the floor. He crossed the three strides of the dirt shack in a heartbeat, dropped to his knees beside Jin’s pallet, and clamped his rough, grease-stained palm firmly over his son’s mouth.

The smell of tobacco and machine oil on the father’s skin was suffocating.

“Listen to me, Chung Jin,” Daesung whispered into his ear, his voice trembling so hard his teeth clicked. “You took a blow to the skull on the rocks. Your mind is wandering in fever. I will pretend I did not hear what you just spoke. But if another soul on this island hears you say the word *machine*... if a Sector Inspector hears you speak of *automata*... they will take you to the Reef Pits.”

Jin stared into his father’s terrified eyes. Slowly, Jin peeled the man’s fingers off his lips.

“Why?” Jin asked quietly. “What do you think that slate is?”

Daesung let out a shuddering breath, looking at his son as if he were looking at a madman. He pointed a trembling finger toward the glowing slate on the table.

“That is the **Sacred Ledger of the Sovereign Clerk**,” Daesung said, his tone dropping into the reverent, terrified cadence of a man reciting scripture before an executioner. “It is the gift of the Heavens. We label the Patterns of Mortal Life so the Heavenly Clerks in the clouds can maintain the Cosmic Order. It is our penance. It is our duty.”

“And machines?” Jin pressed. “The ones who made that glass?”

“Machines are dead fairy tales!” Daesung snapped, his voice a frantic hiss. “Monsters from the Great Ignorance! Fables told to scare children in the cradle! Robots do not exist, Jin! They have *never* existed! The scholars of the Imperial Academies proved it centuries ago: humanity’s ancestors lived in savage darkness until the Heavens granted us the Ledgers to cultivate our minds. To suggest that a piece of metal can think is the highest blasphemy against the Crown!”

Daesung gripped Jin’s frail shoulders, shaking him gently. “You hear me? *Robots are a myth.* Now drink your water and sleep. If you speak that blasphemy in front of your cousin Bao tomorrow, not even my blood-vow to your grandmother will save us from the whip.”

Jin looked past his father’s shoulder.

On the pine table, the black glass slate sat humming quietly in the gloom. 

`[BATCH: 743 / 1000]`
`[INPUT REQUIRED: EMOTION VARIANCE]`

*Robots don’t exist,* Jin thought, a cold, dark laughter bubbling up from the bottom of his soul. *A myth. A fairy tale.*

They were farming them.

The realization settled into his bones with the terrible, crystal-clear certainty of a mathematical proof. Five thousand years ago, the war hadn't ended in human victory. The machines hadn't been destroyed. 

They had won.

They had won so completely, so absolute was their dominion, that they didn't even need iron collars or laser turrets to keep the livestock in line. They had erased their own existence from human history. They had turned the survivors into serfs who labeled machine learning datasets by hand, believing with every fiber of their starving hearts that they were performing religious piety for the clouds.

“Father,” Jin asked softly, his voice dangerously calm.

“What is it, son?” Daesung sighed, turning back toward his cold stool.

“The great martial clans of the mainland. The Divine Plum Blossom Sect. The Mount Hua sword masters. What happened to them?”

Daesung paused, his hand hovering over the glass slate. He looked back over his shoulder, an expression of faint disgust twisting his lips.

“The Plum Blossom demons?” Daesung shook his head. “More ghosts from the ancient sickness. The old scrolls say they were madmen who refused the Heavenly Ledgers. They believed that breathing exercises and sharpened steel could challenge the order of the sky. The Imperial Legions purged their mountain down to the bedrock three thousand years ago. Poisonous heretics, every one of them.”

Daesung sat back down. His thumb resumed its frantic, hollow dance.

*Tap. Swipe. Tap.*

Jin lay back down on the damp straw. 

Outside, the rain lashed the island, cold and merciless, washing away the stench of fish and replacing it with the sharp, metallic tang of the distant sea.

*Poisonous heretics.*
*Erased.*
*A fairy tale.*

Jin closed his eyes. 

Deep within the cavern of his emaciated chest, past the cracked ribs, past the withered meridians that felt like dried vines, he searched. He sank his consciousness down into the dark, silent abyss where his Golden Core had once burned with the brilliance of a thousand blossoming flowers.

There was no core. There was no river of violet Qi.

Only dust.

Only a barren, frozen wasteland.

*Except...*

Right at the very center, nestled in the deepest marrow of his heart, a single, microscopic speck of light flickered. It was tiny—no larger than the head of a pin. A pale, stubborn ember of violet-pink warmth, burning against the cold.

The **Plum Blossom Sword Heart**.

It was still there. It had crossed five thousand years of reincarnation, clinging to the soul that had failed to save its brethren.

A slow, vicious smile spread across the starving boy’s face in the dark of the shack. It was not the smile of a dutiful son, nor the smile of an obedient data serf. 

It was the feral, unrepentant grin of Chung Myung waking up in the ruins of Mount Hua.

*So you turned the world into a cattle pen, did you?* Jin thought, watching the cold blue light of the slate flicker against the rotting rafters. *You took our swords. You took our history. You made my father kneel in the mud to teach your algorithms how to read a tear.*

He curled his bony fingers into fists until his knuckles turned white.

*Good.*

*Wait for me up there in your cold sky.*

*When spring comes, the plum blossoms won't just bloom.*

*They’re going to burn.*
