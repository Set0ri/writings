# Chapter 4: The Blackmail and the Machine's Eye

The rain stopped an hour before dawn, leaving the village wrapped in a cold, suffocating blanket of gray sea-fog.

I slipped back through the drainage gap beneath our hut's cedar planks, my boots caked in wet volcanic grit. Inside, the quiet, rhythmic breathing of my parents filled the dark room. 

My father lay on his side, his large frame curled around my mother to shield her from the draft that whistled through the floorboards. Even in sleep, his brow was knit with worry. His hands, resting open on the coarse blanket, twitched occasionally—the involuntary spasms of a man whose muscles spent twelve hours a day fighting wet clay and heavy pine logs.

I looked at him for a long time in the shadows.

*Two hundred years ago, people like you ruled empires,* I thought, pulling a dry rag over my shivering shoulders. *Now you starve in the mud so a hollow thing in iron mail can hit a quota.*

I didn't sleep. Sleeping was a luxury for people who didn't have to rebuild an entire martial lineage from the marrow of their bones.

Instead, I sat cross-legged on the straw mat, took a slow breath through my teeth, and began cycling the **Gentle Breath of the Morning Dew**.

*Inhale: cold mist, salt, and the faint tang of pine ashes.*
*Circulate: down through the throat, past the cracked collarbone, into the hollow cavern of the lower abdomen.*
*Compress: igniting the tiny, stubborn violet ember resting against my spine.*

Every cycle sent a pulse of liquid warmth radiating through my chest. It wasn't the roaring river of Qi I had wielded at Kurukshetra. It was a drop. A single, stubborn bead of condensed life-force, no larger than a grain of sand.

But a single spark in dry grass is enough to burn a forest.

By the time the third morning bell clanged from the watchtower, the dull ache in my fifth rib had subsided from a stabbing agony to a manageable throb. I washed my face in the rain barrel outside, tied my father’s small iron stone-chisel to the inside of my right forearm with a strip of leather, and headed down the central lane.

***

The Sector Chief’s residence was the only building in Southfall with a slate-tiled roof. 

It sat on a raised mound of crushed limestone near the northern palisade, flanked by two drying barns and a grain warehouse. While the rest of the village lived in drafty huts smelling of damp thatch, the Chief’s house had solid cedar walls, oiled paper windows, and a heavy oak door reinforced with iron bands.

Two village guards leaned against the porch posts, holding rusted boar-spears. Both were half-asleep, their chins tucked into their wool collars to escape the damp wind.

They didn't even notice me walk up the wooden steps.

I didn't knock. I simply kicked the heavy oak door open with the flat of my bare heel.

*THUD.*

The door swung inward, banging loudly against the interior wall.

Inside, the room was vast and eerily quiet. A large cedar desk dominated the center, stacked neatly with rolls of sheepskin ledgers, brass weights, and small wooden abacuses. A fire crackled in a stone hearth, casting long, dancing shadows across the polished floorboards.

Sitting behind the desk was Uncle Kang.

He didn't jump. He didn't startle. He didn't even drop the goose-quill pen held between his thick fingers.

He simply paused mid-stroke, his eyes lifting from the sheepskin parchment with a smooth, unhurried precision that made the hair on my arms stand straight up.

"Jin," Kang said. 

His voice was deep, resonant, and completely devoid of inflection. It sounded like an actor reciting lines he had rehearsed ten thousand times. 

"You should not be standing. The village apothecary reported that your cranium suffered structural fracture. Returning to rest is the optimal course of action."

*Optimal course of action,* I noted, my inner wolf grinning through the cold. *Not 'how are you feeling, nephew?' Not 'what are you doing here?' Just raw, cold efficiency.*

I didn't answer. I strolled across the room, dragging my muddy boots across his clean cedar floorboards, and pulled a heavy wooden chair up to the front of his desk. 

I turned the chair backward, straddled it, and rested my chin on my folded arms, looking at him with that pleasant, completely disrespectful Mount Hua smirk.

"Nice office, Uncle," I said cheerfully. "Very warm. Very dry. Down in Sector Three, the rain leaks right onto my father’s face while he sleeps. But here? You’ve got pine logs burning like it’s a festival day."

Kang set the quill down on a brass stand. 

His eyes were dark, perfectly centered, and fixed directly on the bridge of my nose. He didn't blink. One second. Two seconds. Three seconds. Exactly four seconds passed before his eyelids fluttered once and reopened.

"Daesung is currently assigned to the third drainage trench," Kang stated evenly. "His labor is required to maintain the perimeter barrier. If you have come to request an exemption on his behalf, the request is denied. Sector Three is running a four percent deficit in defensive earthworks."

"I don't give a damn about your earthworks, Uncle," I said, my voice dropping into a casual, venomous drawl. 

I reached into my shirt.

Kang’s fingers twitched slightly on the desk—a micro-movement, no more than two millimeters, but his knuckles locked like iron clamps.

I pulled out a small, rolled scrap of parchment. 

It was a piece of charcoal paper I had found tucked under the basalt shelf in Gull's Maw at midnight—a duplicate of the patrol roster Kang had handed to the Black Wave Bandits.

I tossed it onto the desk. It unrolled slowly between two stacks of grain ledgers.

Kang looked down at the parchment.

For half a second, the room went so silent I could hear the embers in the hearth crumbling to ash.

"The Gull’s Maw is very scenic at dead low tide," I remarked, resting my cheek on my hand. "The waves make a lovely sound against the basalt. And the Black Wave Bandits are very punctual businessmen. Forty minutes before the tide turns. Isn't that right, Uncle?"

Kang didn't flinch. 

He didn't turn pale. A human politician caught selling out his own kin to cutthroats would sweat, shout, deny it, or reach for a dagger to silence the witness.

Kang did none of those things.

Instead, his eyes unfocused for a fraction of a second. A strange, glassy film seemed to pass over his pupils, like oil on water.

"Observation," Kang muttered, his lips moving without any corresponding warmth in his cheeks. "Subject Chung Jin was categorized as low-threat biological unit. Probability of nocturnal surveillance across coastal cliffs: 0.04 percent. Causal variance unaccounted for."

He focused his eyes back on me. 

"How did you reach the western shelf without triggering the trip-wires?"

I let out a loud, mocking laugh that echoed through the high cedar rafters.

"Trip-wires? You think some frayed hemp string tied to rusted cowbells can keep me out? Uncle, when I was ten years old, I used to sneak into the forbidden plum orchards of Mount Hua past three layers of wind-detecting spirit arrays just to steal peaches! Your little wires are cute, though. Very rustic."

Kang stared at me. 

His head tilted slightly to the right—an angle of exactly fifteen degrees. 

"Mount Hua," Kang repeated. The word seemed to hit an internal barrier inside his skull. "Querying historical database. Sector reference: zero. Clan designation: ancient extinct classification. Status: irrecoverable heresy."

He leaned forward, placing his thick, scarred palms flat on the wood.

"What are your terms, subject?"

"Don't call me subject, you cold piece of lard," I snapped, pointing a finger at his nose. "I’m your nephew. Remember? Family? The sacred blood-line your dear brother Daesung breaks his back to protect every single day?"

Kang’s face didn't move. "Specify requirements for informational suppression."

"Listen closely, because I’m only going to say this once," I said, leaning over the desk until our faces were six inches apart. 

"First: my father doesn't dig the double watch anymore. He works the regular daylight shift, and he sleeps in his own bed at night. 
Second: our household gets double rations of rice and three jars of salt every week, delivered to our door without your fat brat Bao showing up to collect tax. 
Third: you stop choking your son in the dark. If I see another bruise on that idiot’s neck, I won't just tell the village Council about your bandit friends—I’ll take my father’s hunting axe and chop off your fingers one by one."

I smiled, showing all my teeth.

"You understand the terms, Uncle?"

Kang sat motionless. 

Deep inside his chest, beneath the wool tunic and the polished iron mail, I heard it again.

*Huuuummmmm.*

The faint, freezing vibration of a cooling pump running through synthetic veins. A sound no normal human ear could hear, but to the refined senses of the Plum Blossom Sword Heart, it was as loud as a blacksmith’s bellows.

Then, Kang spoke. His voice was smooth, measured, and pleasant—the voice of a doting uncle conceding to a stubborn nephew.

"Terms accepted," Kang said, nodding slowly. "Daesung will be reassigned to light timber duty at midday. A sack of grain and two jars of coastal salt will be delivered to your hut before the evening bell. Equilibrium is restored."

He reached out his hand, offering a handshake to seal the bargain.

I looked at his hand. 

The skin looked real. It had hair, pores, even dirt under the fingernails. But the palm had no heat. It was the temperature of river ice.

I didn't take it.

Instead, I pushed back my chair, stood up, and stretched my scrawny arms until my shoulders popped loudly.

"Good boy," I said, turning my back on him and walking toward the door. "Keep the salt dry, Uncle. I hate wet salt."

I walked out of the office into the gray morning air.

Behind me, through the open doorway, I could feel his gaze. 

Kang didn't return to his ledgers. He didn't pick up his quill. He sat completely still in the center of the warm, dry room, his unblinking eyes tracking the rhythm of my footsteps until I turned the corner into the mud lane.

*He agreed too quickly,* I thought, my jaw tightening as I marched through the puddles.

A human crook would try to negotiate. A human coward would promise anything out of panic, then hire thugs to slit my throat in an alley.

A machine doesn't panic.

A machine calculates the path of least resistance to buy time until the scheduled event.

*The bandit raid is tonight,* I realized, looking toward the dark treeline beyond the palisade. *Two hours before dawn, the Black Wave Bandits are supposed to breach the eastern ditch, burn the barns, and slaughter two elders to generate 'fear and compliance.'*

Kang hadn't conceded to my blackmail. 

He had simply marked my family’s hut as the primary target for the fire.

I reached down to my waistband, touching the cold iron chisel strapped against my forearm. 

*Let them come,* I thought, a manic, feral thrill humming through the marrow of my bones.

*Tonight, Southfall Village is going to learn how to bleed.*
